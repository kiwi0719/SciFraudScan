# Anonymous case — 30 Mann-Whitney comparisons from a published paper

## What this is

30 Mann-Whitney U comparisons as printed in a real, published, peer-reviewed
paper: the U statistic, the two group sizes, and the p-value reported beside
them. Nothing else.

**The paper is deliberately not identified**, at the request of the person who
supplied it, and no copy of it is in this repository. Unlike the other cases
here, there is no published reanalysis of this paper — so naming it would mean
publishing a new analysis of specific researchers' work, which is not what
this benchmark is for. The other cases are named precisely because their
verdicts were already established in the peer-reviewed literature.

Comparisons are labelled `comparison_01` … `comparison_30`; the parameters
they compare are not recorded.

## This is NOT a false-positive control

An earlier version of this file called it one. That was wrong, and the mistake
is worth recording because it is easy to make.

Calling a case a false-positive control asserts that the data are honest. The
integrity of this paper has **not been independently established** — there is
no reanalysis, no editorial outcome, nothing. Treating "29 of 30 passed" as
evidence that the checks have a low false-positive rate, when the only reason
to think the paper is sound is that those 29 passed, is circular.

A false-positive control needs ground truth from outside the tool. The
Wansink and Sato cases have it: a published reanalysis examined every cell and
recorded which ones it found consistent. This case has none.

## What it is good for

Two things, and only two.

**1. It exercises the one-sided logic of the Mann-Whitney check.** Ties shrink
p and cannot be recovered from a published U, so the no-tie value is an upper
bound: a reported p *below* it is unremarkable and must not be flagged. Five of
these rows report a p well below the bound, by up to two orders of magnitude,
and the check stays quiet on all five. That behaviour is correct by
construction, not because the paper is honest.

**2. It is real input.** The numbers were not generated by
`benchmarks/generate_examples.py` with a seed chosen by the same person who
wrote the checks. Every synthetic control in this repository shares an author
with the thing it is testing; this one does not.

## Internal consistency is not integrity

Worth stating plainly, because this case invites the confusion: the checks
here test whether reported numbers are **arithmetically consistent with each
other**. They detect carelessness, not competence. Summary statistics computed
by software from fabricated raw data are internally consistent, and would
clear every check in this file.

A `clear` from these checks means "no reporting error of this kind was found".
It does not mean the work is sound, and must never be reported as if it did.

## Result: 29 of 30 internally consistent

29 reported p-values are reachable from the U and group sizes printed beside
them; most match to three or four significant figures.

One is not:

| | |
|---|---|
| U | 2291.0 |
| group sizes | 54 v 54 |
| reported p | 2.94 × 10⁻⁵ |
| largest p this U can produce | 3.13 × 10⁻⁷ |

The reported value is about 94× the bound. Ties cannot account for it — they
move p downward. Nor can rounding of U: it would have to be wrong by more than
150.

Explanations that fit include a transcription slip, a correction applied to
this row and not described, or a p carried over from another comparison. **The
toolkit cannot distinguish between them**, and this file does not try to. What
can be said is narrower: the printed p-value cannot be produced by the printed
statistic, and the paper's stated conclusion for that row — a significant
difference — holds under either value, both being far below .05.

## Development note

This case was added because the paper exposed a real gap: the toolkit had no
Mann-Whitney support. It immediately caught a false positive in the new check.
The first version used the exact null distribution as the bound and flagged 6
of these 30 rows; five were the paper having used the normal approximation,
which differs from exact by a few thousandths at these sample sizes. The bound
is now the largest value across the exact distribution and the normal
approximation with and without a continuity correction.

That correction was driven by the arithmetic — the normal approximation is a
form of the test the authors could legitimately have used — and not by a wish
to see the rows pass.
